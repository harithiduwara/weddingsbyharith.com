I am the one you will forget is there

The first camera I used was my father's, and I was not really supposed to have it.

After that I had a PSP with a camera attachment bolted onto the top, which is a genuinely terrible way to take a photograph and did not slow me down at all. I joined the Photographic Society at {{site.about.school}} in grade 8. By grade 9 they were taking me to quizzes with the seniors, and we won Pilibimbu, which is the largest photography quiz competition in the country. A few of my photographs picked up awards around the same time.

What I did not have was a real camera. So I walked to school instead of taking the bus and put the ten rupees away, every day, for nearly three years. In {{site.about.firstCamera}} I bought a Canon EOS 60D. I was in grade 11.

My first paid job was in {{site.about.firstPaidWork}}, a food shoot for Cafe Noir. Birthdays came after that, then events, then a great many events. In {{site.about.weddingsSince}} I bought a Sony A7 III and an 85mm lens and started photographing weddings, which is still mostly what I shoot and still mostly what I shoot with.

I started my {{site.about.degree}} in 2020, which is to say I started it in a pandemic, and graduated in {{site.about.graduated}}. Four years of Computer Science taught me how to sit with a problem for a long time without panicking, and how to build things that keep working when nobody is watching them. Both turn out to be most of this job. I have written almost no software since.

How I actually work

Documentary is not a filter you apply in Lightroom. It is a set of decisions you make on the day: use the light that is in the room rather than the light you brought, keep working while everyone eats, and trust that what is happening in front of you is more interesting than anything you could arrange.

In practice that means I turn up early and I am quiet. I will have walked your venue. I will know roughly what time the sun clears the wall and which corner of the hall goes dark first. You should never have to think about any of it.

Family groups are the exception and I do them gladly. Quickly, in one place, from a list we agree beforehand. Fifteen minutes, not an hour. You will want them in twenty years even if you resent them on the day.

On a full day there are two or three of us, not just me. That is not padding. A Sri Lankan wedding runs in several places at once, and one photographer covering a poruwa at dawn cannot also be with the groom, or in the hall while the room fills. I would rather have a second pair of hands than tell you afterwards what we missed.

I work all over the island and will travel further if you are getting married somewhere else. Poruwa ceremonies before six in the morning, churches in the rain, and one wedding where the generator failed halfway through the speeches and everybody carried on by phone torch. That one was excellent.
